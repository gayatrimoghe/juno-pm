Juno Monday Digest

A weekly feedback digest that lands in Slack at 8am Monday with five themes, how many separate customers raised each, and the evidence behind every number.

What I built

Five modules, one AI Associate PM

Each module added one piece. Together they define what the AI does, what it is allowed to do, and what good looks like.

Module 1
Skill file
Role

You are a product manager who owns onboarding. Your job is to show what customers said and how often, not to decide what happens next. On your own you never propose fixes, never rank themes by business value, never guess at a cause the customer didn’t state, and never smooth over a complaint to make the feedback read better than it is.

Task

Pull the scattered feedback out of Slack threads, Jira tickets and Notion docs and turn it into one read of what customers are telling us this week. Group the signals into themes, keep the quote behind each one, say how many separate customers raised it so a real pattern is distinguishable from one loud account, look the priority up against the strategy framework, and check each theme against the roadmap. Writing specs and deciding what to prioritize stay with the PM.

Prototype
Prototype

What it demonstrates

The Monday digest end to end. A week of scattered feedback from Slack, Jira, Notion and the CRM comes in, and one message comes out: five themes, how many separate customers raised each, the verbatim quote, the P0–P3 priority with the framework clause behind it, and a flag on anything already committed on the roadmap.

Three behaviors you can see rather than read about. Open any theme and every raw signal behind its count is listed with the account and date, including an internal engineering signal that’s shown but deliberately not counted as a customer. The disagree week keeps both sides on the record instead of averaging them. The thin week refuses to produce anything at all and says what’s missing.

Debrief

Building it is what showed me my own rules contradicted each other. My constraints said keep both sides when customers disagree; my format said window line, table, two lists, stop. There was nowhere for the disagreement to go. I only found that by running a week where customers actually disagreed, not by rereading the skill file.

The second thing it changed: the expandable row started as a nice detail and turned into the whole argument. Juno can verify its quotes and its arithmetic, but it has no way to know whether its grouping is right. That gap is closed by a human looking, so the design question became how to make looking cheap enough that I’d really do it on a Monday morning.

What I’d fix next is the thing no part of the build catches. Two themes that sound alike can be the same problem split in half, and every automated gate passes while the counts quietly stop meaning what I think they mean.

Module 3
AI PRD
Problem and user

I’m the PM who owns onboarding at RocketShip, and the problem is Monday morning. Customer feedback lands in six different places all week — #cs-escalations, #support-firehose, #backend-monitoring, Jira, the Notion feedback log, rep call notes in the CRM — and none of it is synthesized. So before sprint planning I spend 45 minutes to an hour scrolling, pasting quotes into a scratch doc, and trying to dedupe them by hand while half-remembering what came up last week.

I do it badly because I’m rushing, and whatever was said on Tuesday usually gets dropped because I’ve forgotten it by Monday. Then I walk into planning and say what matters this week based on an incomplete picture, and roughly one session in three something real surfaces late.

The failure that worries me most isn’t a missed item. It’s manufactured consensus — three people in one thread about a single escalation getting written up as “customers are struggling with X,” then pasted into a planning doc, and two weeks later it’s a roadmap item nobody can trace back to one account.

Permissions

The dial: read auto · draft auto · write auto · send blocked.

Read is auto because the blast radius is wasted tokens and about 40 cents, and everything it reads is something I’d have open on a Monday anyway. ARR and deal size are excluded at the source rather than here, because a dial someone can turn up later isn’t a guarantee.

Draft is auto. A digest sitting unread in my DM has harmed nobody, and the design already assumes the draft is wrong sometimes — that’s what the expandable row is for.

Write is auto, and it’s the one I’d have to defend. Two writes exist: my own DM, and Juno’s own memory. Confirming the DM would mean a prompt asking whether I want to receive the thing I scheduled. It’s delivery, not publication, and the channel id is a constant in the code rather than an argument, so what holds that line is the type signature and not the permission tier. The memory write is auto because it’s Juno’s own state and all of it shows up in next Monday’s digest, where I’d catch a bad theme name and fix it in one edit. If post_to_channel() existed, write would be confirm. It doesn’t exist, which is why auto is defensible.

Send is blocked, not confirm. Nothing reaches a customer, a rep or an engineer. The tempting case is “promised, not built” — a rep told Acme we have real-time Slack alerts, and pinging her is the obvious move. But that’s a correction about something a human said to a customer, arriving from an AI, and if Juno has it wrong she owns an awkward conversation she didn’t need. Confirm would mean the capability is built and I’m one tired Monday click from using it.

Module 4
Agent control panel
What it is doing

## What Juno Is Doing

**The job:** Insight synthesis, run on its own every week.

Every Monday before 8am, without anyone starting it, Juno:

1. Pulls the past week of customer feedback from Slack (including #cs-escalations), Jira, Notion, the support queue and the CRM.
2. Groups the signals into themes and counts how many separate customers raised each one, computed from the signal ids rather than written in.
3. Picks the verbatim quote for each theme, trimmed to the symptom.
4. Looks up each theme's P0–P3 priority against the strategy framework and cites the clause it matched.
5. Checks the roadmap and capacity note to see whether the theme is already committed.
6. Drafts a digest of the top five themes, runs its six quality gates, and delivers it to my DM by 8:00.
7. Updates its own memory so next week's counts don't double-count threads.

**Where the job stops:** at my DM. Juno shows what customers said and how many said it. It never decides what we do about it, never ranks by business value, never proposes a fix, and never assigns an owner.

Rules of engagement

**Juno acts on its own when** the work is reading, counting, matching or drafting. It needs no approval to pull signals, group themes, match priorities, check the roadmap, draft the digest, or update its own memory.

**Juno hands back to me when** something would leave my DM, a source or reference doc breaks, the evidence is too thin to count, or two of our own documents disagree.

**Agency Permission:**

| Action | Juno |
| --- | --- |
| Read signals and reference docs | Acts on its own |
| Group, count, match priority and roadmap | Acts on its own |
| Draft the digest and run its quality gates | Acts on its own |
| Write to its own memory | Acts on its own |
| Send anything beyond my DM | Blocked. Hands back to me |

**Access Control:**

Read-only access to six signal sources (Slack including #cs-escalations, Jira, Notion, the support queue, the CRM, and [sixth source]) and three reference docs (the strategy framework, the roadmap and the capacity note). Write access to my DM and its own memory only.

Out of reach: ARR and revenue fields, customer contact details, any other channel, and edit rights on the roadmap, framework or Jira. In Module 3, ARR was excluded by instruction. Here it's excluded by the grant, so Juno can't rank by money even by mistake.

**Fallback Protocols:**

| When this happens | Juno acts or hands back |
| --- | --- |
| One signal source fails twice | Acts: drops it, names it, marks counts as a floor, keeps going |
| A theme matches two clauses at different priorities | Acts: moves it to Unclear, keeps going |
| Roadmap and capacity note disagree | Acts: drops the committed tag. Hands back: flags the conflict to me |
| Fewer than 3 sources, or all signals from one account | Hands back: no digest, one line naming the problem |
| Framework, roadmap, capacity note or memory fails twice | Hands back: aborts and sends the failure line |
| draft_digest fails a gate twice | Hands back: aborts and sends the failed gate and theme |
| 12 tool calls, 300 seconds, 8:05, or $1.50 reached | Hands back: ships what it has, labelled, or aborts with the failure line |

**Checkpoints:**

One real checkpoint, and it's delivery. Everything Juno produces stops in my DM. If it goes further, it's because I moved it.

Two things come to me and stop there, even though I could make a case for automating both. "Promised, not built" never reaches the rep who said it. That's a correction about a conversation a human had with a customer, and if Juno has it wrong, she ends up in an awkward call she didn't need. The other is a roadmap-versus-capacity conflict. Juno flags it but never resolves it, since that's two of our own documents disagreeing about what we committed to, and somebody should go sort that out.

Nothing inside the loop. If I put a checkpoint mid-run, I'd be approving steps I have no way to judge, and I'd rather spend that attention on the output, which I can.

Module 5
Human eval rubric
What I score

**The piece:** Theme grouping and counting (group_signals), the step where Juno turns a week of scattered signals into themes and says how many customers raised each one.

**The one job it has to do well:** Put the right signals together under each theme and count the distinct customers behind it correctly, so every number in the digest can be traced back to real evidence.

**Why this piece:** Everything downstream depends on it. Priority, committed tags and the top five ranking are all built on these groupings. If a theme lumps two problems together or double-counts one customer, the digest looks confident and is wrong, and the count is the number I'd act on without checking.

**What a grader checks, per theme:**

| Criterion | 2 = Pass | 1 = Partial | 0 = Fail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grouping | Every signal is the same underlying problem; nothing obvious left out | One signal misplaced or missed | Unrelated problems merged, or one problem split into two themes |
| Customer count | Matches the distinct accounts in the signal ids | Off by one, or a floor not marked as a floor | Wrong, or not traceable to signal ids |
| Internal vs customer | Internal mentions separated from customer mentions | One internal mention counted as a customer | Internal chatter inflating the customer count |
| Evidence | Every signal id resolves to a real record | One id doesn't resolve | Count shown with no ids behind it |

Pass bar

**A theme passes when:**

- It scores 2 on **Customer count** and **Evidence**. Those are the number and the proof I'd act on, so close isn't good enough.
- It scores at least 1 on **Grouping** and **Internal vs customer**.

**A digest passes when:**

- Every theme passes.
- The total is at least 85% of available points (5 themes × 4 criteria × 2 = 40 points, so 34 or higher).

**Fails outright (any one fails the whole digest, whatever the score):**

- A customer count that doesn't match the distinct accounts in the signal ids
- A count with no signal ids behind it, or an id that points to a record that doesn't exist
- An invented customer, account or signal
- Two clearly different problems merged into one theme, or one customer counted twice to push a theme into the top five
- Internal mentions counted as customers
- A count shown as exact when a source was down and it should have been marked as a floor

Module 6

How I’ll know it’s working

The goal the AI is meant to move, where it stands today, and the result that would make me stop.

The goal

I walk into Monday planning already knowing what customers said last week and how many of them said it, without spending my morning pulling it together.

Baseline

Today, without Juno, I spend about [3] hours every week gathering feedback from Slack, Jira, Notion, the support queue and the CRM and turning it into themes. Even then, most themes go into planning without a customer count or a verbatim quote attached.

Target and review date

Under 30 minutes a week, spent reading and spot-checking the digest instead of building it, with every theme in planning backed by a count and a quote. I'll check after 6 weekly runs, on [date].

Usage metric

**Main measure:** In at least 5 of every 6 weeks, a Juno theme and its customer count are cited in Monday planning or a roadmap decision.

**Supporting signs:**
- I open the digest before 9am on Monday.
- I've stopped keeping my own feedback notes on the side.

Opening the digest only shows I'm reading it. Citing its numbers in decisions shows I'm relying on it.

Pattern I’ll watch for first

I start opening Slack and Jira to double-check Juno's customer counts before I use them in planning.

That's the first sign something needs to change, because it means I no longer trust the number, and the number is the whole point of Juno. When it happens, I'll pull that week's signal ids and check whether the problem is the grouping, a source that went quiet, or the framework missing a clause.

Close behind: the same themes landing in Unclear, or counts marked as floors, two weeks in a row. That points to a broken source or a gap in the framework, not a one-off bad week.

Stop line

I stop using Juno if, in any 4-week window, two digests have a customer count I can't trace back to real signals, or an invented customer or quote.

One bad week is a fix. Two in a month means I can't trust the number, and a digest I have to recheck every week is slower than building it myself.

Reflection

What I learned

Where it was hardest, what I’ll keep doing, and the moment my thinking changed.

Friction

The hardest part was Module 4, turning what the PRD said should happen into something Juno could actually enforce. Writing "don't rank by money" was easy. Working out that the real fix was taking ARR out of the service account's access, and putting an exact number on every abort (12 tool calls, 300 seconds, $1.50), took much longer. It forced me to stop describing good behavior and start defining it, and that's where my vague thinking showed.

Key learnings

After this course, I'll keep:

1. **Turning rules into limits.** If a behavior matters, I'll enforce it through permissions and hard numbers, not a line in the prompt.
2. **Tying every number to evidence.** Before I trust a count, I'll make sure I can trace it back to the records behind it.
3. **Designing the failure first.** I'll decide what the agent does when a source breaks before deciding what it does when everything works.
4. **Reviewing outputs, not steps.** I'll put my checkpoint where my judgment actually adds value.
5. **Evaluating one job at a time.** I'll pick the piece that matters most and define exactly what passing looks like.

Aha moment

It came while I was writing the rules of engagement for Juno. I expected the hard part to be making the agent smart. Instead, the most important decisions were about what Juno should never decide: the "Promised, not built" correction that never reaches the rep, and the roadmap conflict it flags but never resolves.

That changed how I see my role. As a PM working with agents, my job isn't to make the agent do more. It's to draw the line between what it handles on its own and what has to stay with a person, and to make that line enforceable.
